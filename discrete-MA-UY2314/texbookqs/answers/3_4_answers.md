# Answers — Section 3.4

###### 1. Universal instantiation / universal modus ponens
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.1|Question]]

#### From $\forall x (x \cdot 1 = x)$: $7\cdot 1=7$, $0\cdot 1=0$, $(-3)\cdot 1=-3$, $\frac{1}{2}\cdot 1=\frac{1}{2}$, and $10\cdot 1=10$.

###### 5. Universal modus tollens
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.5|Question]]

#### All irrational numbers are real. $2+i$ is not a real number, so $2+i$ is **not irrational**.

###### 6. Universal modus tollens
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.6|Question]]

#### A correct program produces no error messages. This program produces error messages. Therefore, **this program is not correct**.

###### 7. Healthy people and apples
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.7|Question]]

#### **Invalid: converse error.** The premise says that a healthy person eats an apple a day. Keisha eating an apple daily does not imply that she is healthy.

###### 8. Freshmen and writing
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.8|Question]]

#### **Valid by universal modus ponens.** Caroline is a freshman, and every freshman must take a writing course. Therefore Caroline must take a writing course.

###### 9. Graphs with edges
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.9|Question]]

#### **Invalid: inverse error.** “No edges” implies “a degree-zero vertex,” but having an edge does not rule out a degree-zero vertex. A graph can have one edge and also an isolated vertex.

###### 10. Product equal to zero
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.10|Question]]

#### **Valid by universal modus tollens.** If $xy=0$, then $x=0$ or $y=0$. Since neither $x$ nor $y$ is $0$, $xy\ne0$.

###### 11. Cheaters and back rows
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.11|Question]]

#### **Invalid: converse error.** Cheaters sit in the back row, but sitting in the back row does not imply that Monty cheats.

###### 12. Two’s-complement representation
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.12|Question]]

#### **Valid by universal modus tollens.** If an $n$-bit two’s-complement numeral represents a positive integer, then its leading bit is $0$. This numeral’s leading bit is not $0$, so the integer is not positive.

###### 13. Discrete mathematics and logic
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.13|Question]]

#### **Valid by universal modus ponens.** Tarik studies discrete mathematics, so Tarik is good at logic.

###### 14. Compilation errors
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.14|Question]]

#### **Invalid: inverse error.** If compilation produces error messages, then the program is not correct. This compilation produces no error messages. Denying the hypothesis does not prove the program is correct.

###### 15. Sums of rational numbers
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.15|Question]]

#### **Invalid: converse error.** A sum of two rational numbers is rational, but a rational sum need not have two rational summands; for example, $\sqrt2+(-\sqrt2)=0$.

###### 16. Even numbers
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.16|Question]]

#### **Invalid: converse error.** If $n$ is even, then $2n$ is even. But $2n$ is even for every integer $n$, including odd $n$.

###### 17. Infinite series
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.17|Question]]

#### **Invalid: converse error.** Terms tending to $0$ are necessary, not sufficient, for convergence. For example, the harmonic series has terms tending to $0$ but diverges.

###### 18. Infinite series
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.18|Question]]

#### **Valid by universal modus tollens.** A convergent series has terms tending to $0$. Since these terms do not tend to $0$, the series does not converge.

###### 19(a). Rimbaud
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.19|Question]]

#### “No good cars are cheap” is: **if a car is good, then it is not cheap.** The argument is **valid**: the Rimbaud is good, so it is not cheap.

###### 19(b). Simbaru
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.19|Question]]

#### **Invalid: converse error.** A car’s not being cheap does not imply that it is good.

###### 19(c). VX Roadster
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.19|Question]]

#### **Valid by modus tollens.** If the Roadster were good, it would not be cheap. It is cheap, so it is not good.

###### 19(d). Omnex
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.19|Question]]

#### **Invalid: inverse error.** Not being a good car does not imply being cheap.

###### 20(a). Dogs and carnivores
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.20|Question]]

#### The argument is **invalid**. Use a diagram with Aaron outside the set of dogs but inside the set of carnivores. Then “All dogs are carnivorous” and “Aaron is not a dog” are true, while “Aaron is not carnivorous” is false.

###### 20(b). Argument form
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.20|Question]]

#### The form

$$P(a)\to Q(a),\qquad \neg P(a),\qquad \therefore\neg Q(a)$$

is **invalid**. Part 20(a) gives a counterexample: $P(a)$ can be false and $Q(a)$ true while $P(a)\to Q(a)$ remains true. This is the inverse error.

###### 21. People, mice, and mortals
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.21|Question]]

#### **Valid.** The diagram has people inside mice and mice inside mortals. Hence people are inside mortals.

###### 22. Discrete mathematics students
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.22|Question]]

#### **Invalid.** Both discrete mathematics students and thoughtful people are contained in the set of people who can tell valid arguments from invalid ones. This does not show that the first set is contained in the second.

###### 23. Teachers and gods
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.23|Question]]

#### **Valid.** Teachers make mistakes, while gods never make mistakes. Therefore no teacher can be a god.

###### 24. Vegans and meat
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.24|Question]]

#### **Valid.** Vegans are vegetarians, and vegetarians do not eat meat. Therefore vegans do not eat meat.

###### 25. College cafeteria food
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.25|Question]]

#### **Invalid.** College cafeteria food is not good, while good food is not wasted. The two premises give no condition on whether non-good food is wasted.

###### 26. Polynomial functions
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.26|Question]]

#### **Valid.** Polynomial functions are differentiable, and differentiable functions are continuous. Therefore polynomial functions are continuous.

###### 27. Logic puzzles
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.27|Question]]

#### **Valid.** “Nothing intelligible puzzles me” means intelligible $	o$ does not puzzle me. Since logic puzzles me, logic is not intelligible by modus tollens.

###### 28. Tarski world: gray objects
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.28|Question]]

#### Reorder the premises as follows:

$$\text{gray}\to\text{circle}\to\text{right of all blue objects}\to\text{above all triangles}.$$

The first implication is the contrapositive of “not a circle $	o$ not gray.” Hence all gray objects are above all triangles.

###### 29. Tarski world: squares
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.29|Question]]

#### Reorder the premises as follows: (3.4.29)

$$\text{square}\to\text{above all black objects}\to\text{right of all triangles}\to\text{above all circles}.$$

The first implication is the contrapositive of “not above all black objects $	o$ not a square.” Hence all squares are above all circles.

###### 30. Tarski world: black objects
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.30|Question]]

#### Reorder the premises as follows: (3.4.30)

$$\text{black}\to\text{square}\to\text{above all gray objects}\to\text{above all triangles}\to\text{above all blue objects}.$$

Here square $	o$ above all gray objects is the contrapositive of the second premise. Thus every black object is above all blue objects.

###### 31. Animals in the yard
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.31|Question]]

#### Reorder the premises as follows: (3.4.31)

$$\text{yard}\to\text{mine}\to\text{trusted}\to\text{admitted to study}\to\text{begs}\to\text{dog}\to\text{gnaws bones}.$$

Thus all animals in the yard gnaw bones. “Unless they beg” gives admitted to the study $	o$ begs, and “only dogs” gives begs $	o$ dog.

###### 32. Logic examples
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.32|Question]]

#### Reorder the implications:

$$\text{not regular order}\to\text{not understood}\to\text{grumbling}\to\text{headache}\to\text{not easy}.$$

The second implication is the contrapositive of “without grumbling $	o$ understood,” and the last is the contrapositive of “easy $	o$ no headache.” Therefore these examples are not easy.

###### 33. Birds in the aviary
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.33|Question]]

#### Birds at least 3 feet tall are in the large aviary, and birds under 2 feet are in the small one. A bird that is at least 3 feet is not under 2 feet, so it is in the large aviary.

###### 34. Shakespeare and Hamlet
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.34|Question]]

#### Reorder the statements:

$$\text{wrote Hamlet}\to\text{true poet}\to\text{stirs the human heart}.$$

Shakespeare wrote *Hamlet*, so he is a true poet and stirs the human heart. As a writer who stirs the human heart, he cannot be a writer who does not understand human nature; hence he understands human nature. Therefore **Shakespeare is clever**.

###### 35. Universal modus tollens
[[3_4_textbook_exercises#Question 3.4.35|Question]]

#### Suppose $\forall x\,(P(x)\to Q(x))$ and $\neg Q(a)$ for a particular $a$. By universal instantiation,

$$P(a)\to Q(a).$$

Modus tollens with $\neg Q(a)$ gives $\neg P(a)$. Therefore universal modus tollens is valid.

###### 36. Universal elimination

#### From $\forall x\,(P(x)\lor Q(x))$ and $\neg Q(a)$, universal instantiation gives $P(a)\lor Q(a)$, and elimination then gives $P(a)$.
