Electricity: Capacitors
Capacitors are combinations of electrical conductors, separated in the circuit. Thus, they represent a "break" in the circuit. If the conductors carry equal but opposite charges, a potential difference exists across them. Although the conductors in a capacitor are separated, they do not interrupt current flow. Instead, their inclusion changes the way in which currents and potential differences are characterized in circuits.

Energy Stored in a Capacitor
As we have discussed, capacitors consist of separated conductors within a circuit. However, capacitors do not allow continuous current flow across the gap. Instead, when a capacitor is connected to a circuit, as current flows in the rest of the circuit, charge builds up on the plates. This allows capacitors to be used to store energy in a circuit.
Think about starting off with a neutral capacitor. To charge it, electric charge must be somehow moved from one plate to another, causing one to become more positive and the other to become more negative. Typically, this happens by connecting a potential source, like a battery, to the capacitor. We know that at some point, the charge will reach a maximum, defined by the following.
Q = CΔV
If a charge moves from one plate to the other, how should this affect the potential difference?
    
✓ Correct. Work is required to move charge from the higher to lower potential. Once charge begins being transferred from the higher potential to the other conducting plate, a potential difference starts to develop. As more charge is added, the potential difference increases.
As more and more charge is transferred from one plate of the capacitor to the other, it takes more work for subsequent charge to transfer. The work done in transferring charge across the capacitor is termed the electric potential energy. This is a measure of the energy stored by the capacitor and is defined as follows.
