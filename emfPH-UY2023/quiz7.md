### Electricity: Electric Potential III (Electric Field from the Potential)

We know from previous lessons that we have a relationship between the electric potential and the electric field. Recall that this relationship was expressed as

> ΔV = −∫ **E** · d**r**.

In this lesson, we'll be looking at the inverse of this relationship; that is, how we can determine the electric field by measuring the electric potential. We'll also look at a way to visualize electric potential due to charged particles.

### Part 1 of 5 — Step 1

It's useful for us to be able to define the inverse of this relationship, as we have tools that make it very easy to measure the electric potential at different locations in space. If we know the electric potential, we can then find the electric field. The device one would use to measure the electric potential is called a voltmeter.

Recall that

> ΔV = −∫ **E** · d**r**

and

> dV = −**E** · d**r**.

So, a more general way of writing this would be

> E_s = −dV/ds

where *ds* is an element of distance in any direction, and *E_s* is the electric field component along that direction. So, if we have an electric field with only one component (e.g., *E_x*), then we can say

> E_x = −dV/dx.

### Part 2 of 5 — Step 2

Consider a charged particle at the origin of a coordinate system. The electric potential along the *x*-axis due to the charged particle is

> V = (1/(4πε₀))(q/x).

What is the electric field along the *x*-axis?

- E_x = q/(4πε₀x)
- E_x = −q/(4πε₀x²)
- E_x = q/(4πε₀x²) *(submitted and correct)*

Let's rewrite this with *k_e*. Substituting in

> k_e = 1/(4πε₀),

we get

> E_x = k_eq/x².

Does this make sense? If we recall the expression for the magnitude of the electric field of a point charge, it was

> E = k_eq/r².

So here we have worked backwards to get *E_x* from *V*.

Now consider a large, uniformly charged surface, lying in the *xy*-plane of a coordinate system, with a surface charge density (charge per unit area) of σ. The electric potential at a point a distance *z* away from the surface is given by

> V = (σ/(2ε₀))z.

Use this to find the *z*-component of the electric field due to this charged sheet, *E_z*.

- E_z = −σ/(2ε₀) *(submitted and correct)*
- E_z = −(σ/(2ε₀))(1/z²)
- E_z = σ/(2ε₀)

So, the electric field is constant.

### Part 4 of 5 — Step 4

The last step is to look at what happens when we use the equation

> ΔV = −∫ **E** · d**r**

and integrate over a closed loop. First, let's define the integral over a closed loop by replacing the symbol.

> ΔV = −∮ **E** · d**r**

We find that when we integrate the electric field over closed loop, the potential difference is

> ΔV = −∮ **E** · d**r** = 0  
> ΔV = 0

Let's look at a few examples of this. Firstly, one can conceptually understand that if we choose the closed loop to be on an equipotential surface, *V* doesn't change along the surface and so ΔV = 0.

Secondly, we look at a closed path of the shape seen below.

![Closed path near a positive point charge](quiz7-assets/q5-step4-closed-path.png)

*Figure description: A small circle labeled + represents a point charge. Six arrows begin at evenly spaced points on the circle (60° apart) and extend radially outward. A dashed blue circle centered on the charge is labeled r = r₁. A larger dashed blue circle, also centered on the charge, is labeled r = r₂. A directed path consists of four segments shown as red arrows. Segment 1 begins at a point on the inner (r₁) circle and extends radially outward, ending at a point on the outer (r₂) circle. Segment 2 begins at the endpoint of segment 1 and continues on the outer circle, forming an arc of approximately 50°. Segment 3 begins on the outer circle at the endpoint of segment 2 and extends radially inward, ending at a point on the inner circle. Segment 4 begins at the endpoint of segment 3 and continues on the inner circle, ending at the starting point of segment 1.*

We wish to find the path integral

> ΔV = −∮ **E** · d**r**

along the direction indicated along the closed path. We can do this by breaking the path into segments, labeled 1 to 4, and considering the potential differences found along each segment.

Which of the following statements are true? **(Select all that apply.)**

- Segment 1 from *r₁* to *r₂* will give us a negative value for ΔV.
- Segment 1 from *r₁* to *r₂* will give us a positive value for ΔV. *(submitted and correct)*
- Segment 2 is along an equipotential surface, so this will give us a positive value ΔV.
- Segment 2 is along an equipotential surface, so ΔV along this segment is zero. *(submitted and correct)*

So, segment 1 has a positive value for ΔV. Let's call this value λ.

What is true for the last 2 segments? **(Select all that apply.)**

- Segment 3 from *r₂* to *r₁* will give us a positive value for ΔV, so ΔV = λ.
- Segment 3 is equal in magnitude to segment 1 but it will be negative, ΔV = −λ. *(submitted and correct)*
- Segment 4 is along an equipotential surface, so V along this segment is zero. *(submitted and correct)*

Adding all these up, we get

> ΔV_loop = segment 1 + segment 2 + segment 3 + segment 4  
> ΔV_loop = λ + 0 − λ − 0  
> ΔV_loop = 0

Although we won't prove it here, it can be shown that any closed path near a single charged particle will give a potential difference of zero. Since electric potential obeys superposition, if the closed path integral is zero for one charge, it will be zero for any distribution of charges. The fact that ΔV over a closed path is zero reflects that electric forces are conservative, and that the potential difference between any two points does not depend on the path.

### Part 5 of 5 — Conclusion

- We can use our previous knowledge of ΔV = −∫ **E** · d**r** to define E_r = −dV/dr.
- A plot of *V* vs. position will give us a graph where the slope represents the electric field, *E*. Because in practice *V* is easier to measure directly (with a voltmeter) than *E*, this gives us a useful way to find electric field.
- Equipotential surfaces are regions in space where *V* is constant along the surface and *E* is constant along the surface. The shapes of these surfaces depend on the charge distribution.
- For a closed loop, ΔV = −∮ **E** · d**r**.
